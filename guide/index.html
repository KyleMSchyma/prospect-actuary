The one-paragraph version

Both boards answer one question: "How likely is this player to become a useful fantasy starter in his first three NFL seasons?" The pre-draft grade answers it with college data only, before anyone is drafted. The post-draft grade answers it again once we know where he was picked and where he landed.

The big lesson: each new piece of real information beats the clever stuff that came before it. Draft position beats college stats, so the post-draft grade is the more accurate of the two.

Shared ideas

  • Tiers. "Hitting" means at least one season in years 1-3 inside a tier. For QBs and RBs the tiers are top 8, top 16 and top 24 at the position. For TEs they are top 6, top 12 and top 18 (a 12-team league starts only 12 TEs, so a TE16 or TE24 season is below replacement). For WRs they are top 12, top 24 and top 36 (more WRs start in fantasy lineups).
  • Fair testing. Each draft class is predicted only with classes whose results were already known at the time, like a forecaster who is not allowed to peek at the future. This is called rolling-origin testing, and it's the honest score.
  • Always compare to a simple benchmark. Every model is measured against "just use the draft pick." If a fancy model can't beat that, we say so.
  • Elastic net. The workhorse model. Think of it as a regression that is told "use as few inputs as you can, and keep their effects modest." It keeps the model from over-reacting to quirks in a small sample, which matters when there are only about 14 draft classes to learn from.
  • Probabilities that make sense together. The models are built so a player's chance of a top-8 season can never be higher than his chance of a top-24 season.
  • Letter grades. Every player's model score is placed on one fixed A+ to F scale per board, so an A means the same thing in 2012 as in 2026.
Rookie grades
1

The pre-draft (college-only) model

What it predicts. Before anyone is drafted, each college player's chance of a top, top-two and any-tier season in his first three NFL years. It's trained on every draft-eligible college player (about 900 to 2,800 per position), so "never made the NFL" counts as a miss. Uses no draft pick and no combine.

Final model in plain words

How to explain it: "It's a scouting report built only from the box score and recruiting rankings. It finds real signal (it ranks future hits well above average) but the NFL draft still knows more."

  • QB, RB: an elastic net on college production, age, recruiting and conference level.
  • TE: the average of an elastic net, a random forest and xgboost on the same inputs (the elastic net was the TE choice until the TE tiers moved to 6/12/18 on 2026-11-12).
  • WR: a plain logistic regression on five inputs (receiving yards, dominator share, years out of high school, recruiting rating, Power 5). It tied the elastic net, and the simpler model wins ties.
  • Random forests, gradient boosting (xgboost) and an average of models were all tested and lost everywhere except TE. With only a few dozen hits per position, simpler models are more reliable.

Key drivers. Youth (fewer years out of high school), recruiting rating, Power 5, and the position's production: QB accuracy, TD rate and low INT rate; RB touchdown share and receiving; WR receiving yards and dominator share; TE career receiving yards and recruiting rating.

FCS adjustment. FCS stats are shrunk toward an FBS scale. Players who transferred from FCS to FBS saw their production share drop more than FBS-to-FBS transfers, which gives a factor of about 0.69 for receiving and rushing and 0.79 for passing. Conference labels were tested as extra inputs (including a credibility-weighted conference effect) and did not help.

Class grades. Add up the expected tier hits of each class's top prospects (QB 12, RB 20, WR 32, TE 15) and grade against 2010-2023 (A = top 10%, F = bottom 10%). Current: 2027 projected A− overall, 2026 F, 2025 A, 2024 C. Class grades track reality modestly (correlation about 0.5 for the overall grade), so trust the overall grade more than any single position's.

2

Pre-draft player grades

How to explain it: "An A means the college résumé looks like past hits. It's a strong flag at the top, not a guarantee, and a low grade doesn't rule a player out."

What it is. A letter grade (A+ to F) for every drafted QB, RB, WR and TE from 2010-2026, plus the top 2027 prospects, from the college-only model. Historical grades are scored by a model that never saw that class (it did see later classes, so the hit rates below are slightly optimistic next to the rolling-origin test).

The result. A-range players hit about 59% of the time, B about 28%, C and below about 13%. The grade separates the top well but not the bottom (C, D and F hit at similar rates). Draft slot is still more accurate, and the grade only adds to draft slot for QBs. Famous misses: Johnny Manziel (A+), N'Keal Harry (A+); famous low grades that hit: Josh Allen (F), Cooper Kupp (F), Tyreek Hill (D), George Kittle (D).

Post-draft player grades

How to explain it: "The pre-draft grade is what the college stats say. The post-draft grade adds what the NFL said with its pick. When they disagree, history sides with the pick more often, which is why a pre-draft A who goes in round 6 usually fades."

What it is. The same A+ to F board for every drafted player from 2010-2026, but built from the post-draft model: the college profile plus draft pick and landing spot (how much target, touch or attempt share the new team has open, and how good the incumbents are). It sits next to the pre-draft grade, so you can see how much draft capital moved each player and each class. 2027 isn't on it because nobody has been drafted.

Final model in plain words. An ordered model: picture a ladder with rungs at top 8, top 16 and top 24 (top 6, 12 and 18 for TEs; top 12, 24 and 36 for WRs). One score per player says how high up the ladder he's likely to climb. It's fit as an elastic net, and the biggest single input is the draft pick (as its log, because the gap between picks 1 and 10 matters far more than between 150 and 160).

The result. Post-draft A-range players hit 66% of the time, B 34%, C 10% and D/F 4%. Unlike the pre-draft grade, it separates the bottom too. It also ranks players within a class better (average rank correlation 0.49-0.61 vs 0.31-0.45). For QBs it beats draft slot alone; for RBs, WRs and TEs it's about the same as draft slot. Big risers include Carson Wentz (F to A-), Doug Martin (D to A) and Travis Kelce (C to A-); big fallers are mostly late picks with strong college numbers, such as Alex Collins (A- to D).

Comparing across positions: the Value column

How to explain it: "The grade tells you how good a prospect is for his position. Value tells you what he's worth on a dynasty trade market, so you can line up a running back against a quarterback."

Why. A grade is a rank within a position, so an A tight end and an A quarterback aren't worth the same. Both grade boards show Value: each player's expected dynasty trade value on the same 100,000-point scale as the Trade Values page, for the league you set on the Trade Values page (it follows every setting there: teams, scoring, TE premium, superflex, starters, flex, first-down bonus), or the default league (12 teams, superflex, PPR, 2 RB / 3 WR / 1 TE, no flex) if you haven't set one. The All tab is sorted by it.

How it's built. Take his chance of each fantasy tier (top, middle, bottom, none), and for each tier what past drafted players in it actually scored over their first five seasons: games, points per game above the replacement starter, discounted 15% a year and adjusted for his age. Add those up, weighted by the chances, then apply one calibration multiplier (1.11, fit so that past round-1 rookie picks come out at what those players actually produced). It's the same math that prices the 2027 rookie picks, so a 2027 prospect's Value is exactly his rookie-pick value.

Which chances. Post-draft probabilities for drafted players and for 2027 players in the mock draft. For other 2027 prospects, the pre-draft probabilities after a fix: the college-only model is trained on every college player, so its chances include the risk of never being drafted, and among players who were drafted they ran about a third too low (expected any-tier rate 16% vs 25% actual, 2010-2023). A small logistic recalibration per position and tier, fit on drafted players' out-of-class predictions, fixes the level without changing anyone's order within a position. The original pre-draft grades and probabilities are unchanged. For past classes, Value is what the player looked like at draft time in today's league, not what he's worth now.

Redraft Trade Values

How to explain it: "How many more points will this player give me than a waiver pickup, for the rest of the year? Add up both sides of a trade and the bigger number wins."

What it is. A trade value for every QB, RB, WR and TE for the rest of the 2026 season only, on a 100,000-point scale where values add up: two players together are worth the sum of their values.

The formula. Value = (credible points per game − replacement points per game) × expected games left. The best player is set to 100,000 and everyone else is scaled to match.

  • Points over replacement. The replacement player is the last starter at his position in your league, the player you could start instead. Working per game, not in season totals, means QBs don't rise to the top just because they score more points: the replacement QB scores a lot too.
  • Your league's settings. Pick teams (8-14), half or full PPR, a TE premium, 4-6 point passing TDs, a first-down bonus for each position (0 to 1 point per rushing or receiving first down; passing first downs never count), superflex, and how many RB, WR, TE and FLEX spots start. Scoring changes the points themselves, so each scoring setting gets its own fitted model. Lineup settings set the replacement line: every team fills its position slots, then FLEX, then SUPERFLEX, each with the best player left, and the last starter at each position is the line. The default (12 teams, superflex, 2 RB, 3 WR, 1 TE, no flex) gives the QB24, RB24, WR36 and TE12.
  • Credible points per game (shown on the site as "Expected PPG"). A blend of this season and the player's last three seasons, using the actuarial credibility formula Z = games ÷ (games + k). The more games he has played this year, the more this year counts. k was fit for each position by testing on 2016-2025 (weeks 1-9 predicting weeks 10-18); a full-time player's first 4 to 6 games count as much as his history. A player with little history leans on what his draft slot usually produces.
  • Injury and role. Expected games = team games left × his chance of playing each game. That chance is two parts: his role (the share of healthy games he plays, blending this season with the average for his depth-chart spot) and his health (injury-only missed games over this season and the last three, blended with his position's average). Games missed while healthy, because of the depth chart or a benching, do not count against his health. A player on injured reserve or listed Out, Doubtful or Questionable on the last injury report gets the average share of remaining games that players with that status went on to play. Depth charts, the injury report and weekly roster status all come from nflverse.

The result. On 2016-2025, the credible points per game beat both "this season only" and "history only" at every position, and the full value ranked weeks 10-18 better than "current pace × games left" (rank correlation 0.55-0.68 vs 0.47-0.63) with about a third less error. That held for every scoring setting at every position (all 110 position-and-scoring combinations, including every first-down bonus), and for five different league setups from 8-team 1-QB to 14-team superflex (0.61-0.70 vs 0.54-0.66).

Dynasty Trade Values

How to explain it: "It's how an insurer values a pension. Each future season is a payment, weighted by the chance he's still producing to collect it. Young players earn value from their runway, older stars from what they're doing now."

What it is. The same idea stretched over the rest of 2026 and the next five seasons (2027-2031), on the same adding-up 100,000-point scale.

The formula. Value = the sum over each season of: discount × chance he is still playing × expected games × expected points per game over replacement. It is built like an actuarial annuity: each season is a "payment", weighted by the chance he is still around to collect it, and each later season counts 15% less.

  • Potential. Draft slot and years in the league set the starting expectation for young players. As they play, real production takes over through the same credibility blend.
  • Aging. Each position's development and decline is fit from past seasons: next season's points per game from this season's credible points per game, age, years in the league and draft slot. Running backs and receivers fade from their late 20s; quarterbacks hold into their 30s.
  • Staying in the league and injuries. The chance he plays next season and the share of games he plays both come from logistic models with age. For quarterbacks the chance of playing next season and the points projection also use his depth-chart spot and the share of his team's games he has started this season, so a backup is not valued like a starter.
  • Upside near replacement. A player projected just under replacement still gets a little value from his chance of beating it, using the insurance "stop-loss" formula for the expected amount above a threshold. The spread used for each future season is measured from how far past projections actually missed one to five seasons out. It used to be assumed to keep growing every year, which overstated the upside and made the totals run about 20% high.
  • 2027 rookie picks. Until the 2027 class is drafted, the 30 QBs, RBs, WRs and TEs in our 2027 mock draft (Renner) are priced from the post-draft model, using the mock pick as their draft capital and the mock team as their landing spot. Every other prospect is priced from the pre-draft model, which has no draft capital. Each prospect has a chance of each fantasy tier in his first three seasons (QB, RB: top 8 / 16 / 24; TE: top 6 / 12 / 18; WR: top 12 / 24 / 36; or a miss). Each tier is turned into points using what past drafted players in that tier actually did in their first five seasons (2027-2031, the same seasons every player is valued over; 2010-2022 classes): games played, points per game and how spread out they were. One calibration multiplier (1.03 as of November 13, refit each week) is applied on top, fit so that past round-1 rookie picks (2016-2022 classes) come out at what those players actually produced, measured the same way as for players (each season's own replacement line and its real number of games). It rests on 84 players in 7 classes, so it is uncertain: the 95% range is about 0.80 to 1.47. His expected value is the probability-weighted sum, valued against your league's replacement line with the stop-loss upside, discounted from 2027. Points per game are adjusted for his age with the aging model's age curve, against the average rookie's age at his position. The prospects are then ranked for your league: pick 1.01 is worth the best prospect's value, 1.02 the second best, and so on. Every prospect on the board is assumed to enter the draft, and ages are estimated from years out of high school. In the default league, 1.01 (Jeremiah Smith as the mock's first pick, an 86% chance at a top-12 WR season) is worth about the #12 asset (57,000), the rest of round 1 about 19,000-34,000 and round 3 about 12,000-14,000.
  • Does one star equal two good players? These values add with no extra "consolidation" curve, because the replacement line already handles it: in a shallow league the 40th-best player barely beats the waiver wire, so two mid players add up to little. For the default league the values nearly match the trade anchors the site started from (#1 ≈ #8 + #20, #8 ≈ #20 + #40, #20 ≈ #45 + #80): the two-player side comes out 0.99 to 1.12 times the single player.

The result. Starting from week 9 of each season 2016-2020, with models fit only on earlier seasons, the values ranked the next five seasons better than "his current pace continues" (rank correlation 0.70 vs 0.63), at every position and for players in their first three years. That held for every scoring setting and for all five league setups tested (0.61-0.74 vs 0.55-0.67). Predicted totals came out at 0.95 to 1.01 times what players actually produced across the five league setups (counting each past season at its real 16 or 17 games), so players and picks are now on the same footing.

Market check

How to explain it: "It's a second opinion. When our numbers and the market agree, that's reassuring. When they disagree by a lot, one side knows something the other doesn't: a role change, an injury, or production the market hasn't priced in yet. Those are the players worth a closer look."

The Market check page ranks every player by our trade values and by a market source, then shows where the two lists disagree. Our side is the Trade Values page set to a 12-team superflex league with 0.5 PPR, 4-point passing TDs and 2 RB, 3 WR, 1 TE starters. The market side is either Dynasty Daddy (dynasty and redraft trade values from its public API, saved once a week) or DynastyProcess (dynasty values built from FantasyPros expert consensus rankings). Only ranks are shown.

Limits and what we rely on

How to explain it: "It is a careful estimate from public data, with known blind spots. Use it to inform a decision, not to make it for you."

  • What it is for. Comparing players and picks for fantasy football: who is more likely to become a starter, and roughly what a player is worth in a trade in your league. It is not betting advice and it is not a prediction of any single game.
  • Data relied on. College stats and recruiting ratings from CollegeFootballData.com; NFL stats, draft picks, rosters, depth charts and injury reports from nflverse. We use them as published and do not audit them. A wrong birth date, position or depth chart flows straight into the numbers.
  • Main assumptions. Later seasons are discounted 15% a year. Dynasty values count the rest of 2026 through 2031 for everyone. Replacement is the last starter when every team fills its lineup. Until the 2027 draft, a mock draft stands in for real draft slots, and every prospect on the board is assumed to enter the draft, which overstates underclassmen who may stay in school.
  • What the models do not see. Scheme, coaching changes, contracts, trades that have not happened, off-field issues, and anything a scout sees on film. Role is seen only through the weekly depth chart and injury report, so a change between updates is missed until the next Tuesday.
  • How exact the numbers are. Values are shown to the nearest 100 and are estimates. Two totals within about 10% of each other are too close to call. Rookie pick values are rougher than player values: their calibration multiplier is 1.03 with a 95% range of about 0.73 to 1.34.
  • Small samples. The models learn from the 2010 to 2023 draft classes, and that is not many players: only 24 quarterbacks and 30 tight ends have earned an A-range post-draft grade. Hit rates for a single grade can move several points with one more class.
  • Not valued yet. Jayden Higgins, Travis Hunter and Brandon Aiyuk are missing from the trade values. A missing name is a gap, not a zero.

Actuarial concepts behind it

The methods come from the Society of Actuaries' predictive analytics exams (ATPA and PA).

If someone asks...Point to
"How do you know it isn't overfit?"Rolling-origin validation by draft class; fixed benchmark; bootstrap confidence intervals
"Why not a fancier model?"Model comparison (ATPA 4.5): random forest and xgboost lost to a penalized GLM on small samples
"Why does the model say what it says?"Permutation importance, PDP/ICE and SHAP (ATPA 4.3)
"What about missing college data?"Leakage-safe imputation with missing flags (ATPA 3.7a)
"Does conference strength matter?"Credibility / mixed-model test (ATPA 3.3), no gain
"Why are probabilities nested?"Ordered (cumulative logit) models and forced monotone probabilities
"How do you blend this year with past years?"Bühlmann credibility: Z = n ÷ (n + k), with k fit on past seasons
"How do you value future seasons?"A life annuity: discount × survival probability × expected points over replacement
"Why does a backup still have some value?"Stop-loss: the expected amount above a threshold when outcomes are uncertain
"What is a draft pick worth?"Expected value over outcome tiers (like a claim-severity mix), with the Nth-best prospect setting pick N
